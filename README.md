## 2. What Is ADM?

Automated Decision-Making (ADM) is the use of computer systems to make, or help make, decisions that affect people. The Commonwealth Ombudsman defines an automated system as “a computer system that automates part or all of an administrative decision-making process” (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2025, p. 7).

ADM includes:

- **Rule-based systems:** Apply predefined rules, such as calculating benefit eligibility.
- **Data-matching systems:** Compare records from different sources, as Robodebt did with tax office income data.
- **Machine learning models:** Predict risk scores, such as identifying claims for possible fraud.

A system may make the final decision, recommend a decision to a human officer, or automate only part of the process (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2025, p. 7).

## 3. Why ADM Matters to Me as an ICT Student

Government ADM is different from many commercial software systems because decisions can affect people's income, housing, education and freedom. One flawed rule can affect thousands of people, especially vulnerable members of society.

The ethical ideas from this unit help me understand these risks:

- **ACS Code of Professional Conduct:** Primacy of the Public Interest, Honesty, Competence and Professionalism guide ICT practitioners to act responsibly.
- **Kantianism:** People should be treated as individuals with dignity, not merely as a means to reduce costs.
- **Act and rule utilitarianism:** The benefits of efficiency must be weighed against the total harm a system may cause.
- **Social contract theory:** Government decisions should follow fair rules that protect citizens and allow them to challenge decisions.
- **Virtue ethics:** ICT professionals should demonstrate honesty, courage, diligence and care.

## 4. ADM in Australia and Overseas

My artefacts explore automated decision-making in Australia, the United Kingdom and the Netherlands. They demonstrate how systems intended to recover money or identify fraud can produce unfair outcomes.

| Case | Purpose | Main ethical concern |
|---|---|---|
| Australia: Robodebt | Recover welfare overpayments | Unlawful debts and reversal of the burden of proof |
| United Kingdom: DWP fraud model | Flag Universal Credit claims for possible fraud | Differences in outcomes across demographic groups |
| Netherlands: DUO student finance | Select students for fraud checks | Indirect discrimination linked to migration background |

These cases show why technical performance alone is not enough to establish that an automated system is fair.

## 6. Artefact 2: Robodebt as a Socio-Technical Failure (2024)

**Source:** [Robodebt: A Socio-Technical Case Study of Public Sector Information Systems Failure](https://doi.org/10.3127/ajis.v28.4681)

**Insert screenshot of the journal article here.**

### Summary of the artefact

This peer-reviewed case study analyses Australia's automated debt-recovery scheme, which raised debts against approximately 433,000 people (Clarke, Michael & Abbas, 2024, p. 1). The scheme used income averaging and required recipients to prove that they did not owe the alleged debts (Clarke, Michael & Abbas, 2024, pp. 15, 17).

### Why I chose this artefact

I chose this article because it presents Robodebt as a socio-technical failure rather than simply a programming mistake. As an ICT student learning to build software, I understand that meeting technical requirements is not enough. Developers must consider the assumptions behind a system and the people affected by its outputs. The article's discussion of manual review for complex cases (Clarke, Michael & Abbas, 2024, p. 21) connects with the need for meaningful human review and fair treatment.

### Summary of the artefact

Following Robodebt, the Attorney-General's Department consulted the public on a consistent framework for government ADM. The consultation received 41 submissions and 20 survey responses. Stakeholders called for transparency, meaningful explanations, risk assessments, ongoing auditing and clear pathways for human review (Attorney-General's Department, 2025, pp. 1, 3).

### Why I chose this artefact

I chose this document because it shows Australia responding to Robodebt at a policy level. Ethical ICT practice is not only about individual developers; it also depends on the rules that society establishes. This connects with social contract theory because citizens should be able to trust government systems to follow fair rules. Appropriate regulation, transparency and review can help protect people affected by ADM.

## 9. Artefact 5: Bias in the UK's AI Benefits-Fraud System (2024)

**Source:** [Computing — AI Bias Uncovered in UK Welfare System](https://computing.co.uk/news/2024/ai/ai-bias-uncovered-in-uk-welfare-system)

**Insert screenshot of the source here.**

### Summary of the artefact

The UK's Department for Work and Pensions uses machine learning to flag Universal Credit claims for possible fraud. An internal fairness analysis released under Freedom of Information found statistically significant differences in outcomes by age, disability, marital status and nationality (Kundaliya, 2024). The department stated that a human caseworker still makes the final decision.

### Why I chose this artefact

I chose this case because it closely relates to the ethical risks of an AI model that meets its technical goals but treats groups unfairly. It made me question whether human involvement provides genuine oversight or simply approves automated recommendations. The ACS Code's principles of Competence and the Primacy of the Public Interest support appropriate bias testing and responsible disclosure of risks.

## 10. Artefact 6: Algorithmic Discrimination in Dutch Student Finance (2024)

**Source:** [Erasmus Magazine — Discrimination by DUO Even Worse Than Thought](https://www.erasmusmagazine.nl/en/2024/05/24/discrimination-by-duo-even-worse-than-thought/)

**Insert screenshot of the source here.**

### Summary of the artefact

The Dutch student finance agency DUO used a risk-scoring algorithm to select students for fraud checks, considering factors including level of study, age and distance from their parents' home. Students with a non-European migration background were selected for home visits 6.2 times more often than other students. The government apologised for indirect discrimination (Erasmus Magazine, 2024).

### Why I chose this artefact

I chose this case because it shows that unfair outcomes can arise even when ethnicity is not directly included as an input. Variables that appear neutral may still act as proxies for a person's background. As an ICT student, I have learned that removing a sensitive field does not automatically make a system fair. Developers should test how decisions affect different groups and investigate unexpected patterns. Virtue ethics highlights the diligence and care needed to prevent harm.
